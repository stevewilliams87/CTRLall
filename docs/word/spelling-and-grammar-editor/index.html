    Spelling and Grammar (Editor)

    ⭐ Beginner Level

    Word features automated tools to review all the content in your document. While you should always read through a document you’ve written, these tools provide a quality standard checker of the spelling and grammar in your document before you publish and distribute it.

    In Word 365 and newer Word versions the Review tab on the ribbon has two reviewing tools: Editor and Spelling and Grammar.

    Editor is the new tool while Spelling and Grammar is an older version. The major difference is that the latter works offline. Use Editor if you can as it has more features.

    How to use Editor

    In the Review tab click on Editor and a pane appears on the edge of the app screen. Here you can choose the writing style and check the spelling and grammar in your document. There are other features as well which are explored in the intermediate version of this artile.

    Click on the red dots below to learn what each option does:

    Spelling checker

    Click on Spelling and Editor will now go through all the spelling errors it has identified in your document one by one. For each it'll present you with the queried spelling in red in a preview box; below the preview under Consider: it will suggest potential correct spellings to change it to. There's an audio button to the right to have Editor read out what it thinks is wrong and the sentence it sits in.

    If you click on a suggested spelling it will be updated to that on the page.

    Note: You don't have to take the suggestion. You can tell Word to ignore what it's querying and click Ignore once further below. If you don't want Editor to keep highlighting that spelling throughout the document you can click Ignore all.

    Another option is to add that queried spelling to Word's dictionary via Add to dictionary. If you have a term commonly used in your work/field you might want to add this to the dictionary so Word stops querying it.

    Grammar checker

    Click on Grammar and Editor will do the same bringing up grammatical queries one by one. Each instance queried is shown in the preview in blue and you can use the audio button to have Editor read out what it thinks is wrong grammatically with the sentence.

    Below the preview it will give suggestions for you to decide on. Click on it to make the change or choose Ignore once to disregard that instance it has queried. If you want it to disregard the grammar rule through the document click Stop checking for this.

    Limitations – What Editor can’t do

    You can't rely on a spell checker to do all the work for you. Editor is good at picking up on spelling errors and most grammatical slip ups but it isn't going to pick up everything. It can't really detect an issue with something that requires context to understand or fix.

    Personally I always re-read what I've written to make sure it makes sense. Always read through a document yourself before you call it finished.

    Some examples of Editor's limitations are listed in the intermediate version of this article.

    Editor vs Spelling and Grammar

    You may have noticed on the Review tab there's two types of spelling and grammar tools in Word: Editor & Spelling and Grammar. What's the difference?

    • Editor is the modern reviewing tool introduced in newer versions of Word. It reviews a document’s spelling, grammar, style, clarity and formality and is AI-assisted.
    • Spelling and Grammar is a legacy label from older versions of Microsoft Office that still lives in the ribbon. It gives you a slightly narrower scope: spelling only, or, spelling and grammar but without as much clarity and extras as Editor provides.
    • However, Spelling and Grammar runs on the same engine as Editor. It’s essentially the same feature with an old front door left unlocked.

    So what’s the point of having Spelling and Grammar?

    The answer is if you’re offline. Editor requires an internet connection to work because of its AI component. Spelling and Grammar is left in to give you an automated reviewing tool if you’re without an internet connection.

    So in conclusion: Use Editor, unless you’re offline in which case use Spelling and Grammar.

    For this article we’re going focus on Editor. If you have an older version of Word then you won’t have this feature but Spelling and Grammar follows the same processes enough for you to be able to pick it up.

    Limitations – What it can’t do

    At the end of the day you should read and proofread your own work. I come from an Editor background so I’ll always advocate for that. At the end of the day it’s a computer program, not a human, and there’s a limit to what it can understand and do.

    Here’s some examples of where the checker can miss or confuse something and what you need to look out for checking content yourself:

    Missing words

    Example: ‘She went store.’

    Problem: Missing ‘to the’ before ‘store’. Editor is usually good at detecting missing words but dropp the odd word, especially in a longer sentence, and it can get missed.

    Numbers, dates, and units that are valid but wrong

    Tense or number agreement across a long sentence:

    Example 1: ‘The list of items that were purchased was reviewed.’

    Issue: Here it's got the subject and verb confused and anchored the verb to the wrong noun (‘items’ vs ‘list’).

    Example 2: ‘Each of the students have submitted their assignment.’

    Issue: Here ‘each’ needs the singular verb ‘has’, but ‘have’ slides through instead because it agrees with the nearer plural noun ‘students’.

    Tone/register mismatches

    Example: ‘don't’ and ‘can't’ in a formal document.

    Issue: Casual contractions in formal writing like ‘don't’ and ‘can't’ may not be noticed. It’s not wrong grammatically but Editor's formality suggestions can be hit-or-miss at consistently flagging every instance.

    Ambiguous pronoun references

    Example: ‘John told Dave he was wrong.’

    Issue: This is grammatically perfect, but ‘he’ in that sentence is ambiguous. Who is John referring to? This is about context and needs actual comprehension.

    Set custom grammar settings in Word

    You can choose custom grammar settings for Edtior to look for, a lot of which aren't turned on by default. Here's how to find them:

    1. Go to File > Options.

    2. In the Word Options dialog select Proofing.

      Make sure Writing Style is set to Grammar & Refinements, then click on Settings next to it.

    3. On the Grammar options dialog scroll and select/deselect what you need, e.g. there is a whole section of Inclusiveness that is turned off by default for criteria like gender bias, ethnic/racial bias, age bias, etc.
